Holidaying is a skill. We've all been on those holidays where we have tried to tick off items from a list, only to realise later in life that it's not worth it. Then, there are some holidays that leave you with a new perspective and actual stories to tell.

I went to Cornwall in July, and on my last day there, I picked up a book called *The Swordfish and the Star* by Gavin Knight. It has stories of people and fishing from Cornwall's coast, and it caused fiction-like hallucinations as I turned the pages.

It told me stories about the fisherfolk of this region. I felt like I was right there with them on the boats, fighting poverty in a rich country, reveling in the adventure and battling a sea that didn't care about economics.

Cornwall morphed from where I holidayed to a microcosm of all of Earth + humanity happening in one tiny area. Dolphins swimming underneath the water, pilchards reflecting the sunlight and getting caught on the shores. Pilchards getting overfished, then getting re-branded to Cornish sardines and enriching new entrepreneurs. Tin and copper enriching the ocean, whales recycling nutrients with their poo. Fishermen fishing, big fish eating small fish, big trawlers eating smaller boats and big merchants eating small merchants. I could picture the Cornish people trying to prevent European boats from fishing in these productive waters only to find out that economic prosperity, like the ocean, remained elusive.

As I bubbled out of the book, I could feel the grief and challenges of an area that has seen it all. And I paused to notice the power of storytelling. This re-colouring of memories would not have been possible had the writer narrated it like a typical non-fiction book. It had to read like a novel for it to have that very profound effect.

On my second day in Cornwall, I visited St Michael's Mount. It's a beautiful island with a castle, connected to the mainland via a causeway. It has striking parallels with the Sindhudurg fort in Maharashtra, a fortress built by Shivaji in the 17th century far far away from the coast. Memories came back flooding from the time I went there as a 15 year old. My teenage recklessness had my parents worried. I jumped from stone to stone on the wall of the fort. To one side was the Arabian Sea, and to the other was a huge ditch, but all I cared about was the beauty of this fortress, the expanse of the deep blue sea, the very orange sunset and the very absent orange lifejacket.

Nevertheless, the book got me thinking about the many beautiful stories that Konkani fishermen might have and the history that could be well presented. But I never got to hear them or read them. It's sad that I never got to live Konkan via a book, the way I could live Cornwall.

But I am hopeful. Hopeful because as India grows and re-discovers itself, its history and its depth, great stories will emerge. They have to. Not because they need to pacify the need for an upwardly mobile middle class who now have the means to pay for content. Because, as a close friend of mine always remarks, if we don't tell our stories, who will?
